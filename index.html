Coloring is older than you think

Filling in outlines with color isn't a modern fad. It's a quiet habit people have kept for nearly 150 years — and every generation has found its own reason to pick it back up.

📖
1879

The very first coloring book

Illustrator Kate Greenaway and the publishers McLoughlin Brothers released The Little Folks' Painting Book — outline engravings printed with nursery rhymes beside them. There was no precedent for the idea, so the publishers colored the first plate themselves just to show readers what to do.

🖍️
1903

Color becomes affordable

Crayola's first small box of wax crayons put a full palette within reach of ordinary households. Coloring stopped being a craft for the few and became something almost every child grew up doing.

🌀
Early 1900s

A psychiatrist notices something

Carl Jung drew and colored circular mandalas as a daily practice and encouraged his patients to do the same, treating the repetitive filling-in of a pattern as a way to settle the mind. It's one of the earliest hints that coloring was never only for children.

😏
1961

Grown-ups get their own

The Executive Coloring Book arrived as a satire of office life — a coloring book aimed squarely at adults. It was a joke, but the joke worked because the appeal was real.

🌿
2013

The book nobody knew how to shelve

Scottish illustrator Johanna Basford published Secret Garden, a densely detailed pen-and-ink coloring book for adults. Her publisher printed a cautious first run — the sales team weren't sure whether it belonged in the children's section or the art section. It went on to sell millions of copies worldwide.

🎨
2015

The craze goes global

Adult coloring books climbed bestseller lists around the world, and colorists filled social feeds with finished pages, pencil comparisons and shared tips. What had been a private habit turned into a worldwide hobby.

🐾
Today

Why a detailed page holds you longer

A few reasons people keep coming back to coloring — shared because they're interesting, not as medical claims.

🧠

Two kinds of thinking at once

Coloring blends careful, logical work — staying inside the lines — with open creative choices about color and mood. Both parts are working together.

🌬️

Mindfulness without the effort

Therapists often reach for structured coloring as an easy entry point to mindfulness: a repetitive, present-moment task that can quiet a busy head.

🔎

Detail keeps attention

A fox's kitchen full of jars and hanging herbs holds focus far longer than a simple shape. There's always one more corner to finish.

🕰️

Ten years and still going

Adult coloring has outlasted most relaxation trends of the last decade — a good sign it's answering something real rather than riding a moment.
